§ 2

The core practice

Three questions organize the work, three rules govern change, and every measure is checked for feedback.

Three questions. They overlap with double-loop learning and are practical distinctions, not levels of mind.

¶ Inference: what follows inside the present model?

¶ Criticism: does the model or strategy fit the problem?

¶ Constitution: are the goals, categories, standards or participants themselves wrong?

Regulation and revision. Holding an agreed condition and revising the purpose need different procedures and different speeds. Declare the regulated variable, its bounds, who benefits, who bears its costs and who can change it. A technically effective regulator can enforce an objectionable goal.

Change. Anyone may explore alternatives. Replacing an operating rule needs a stated inadequacy: an anomaly, an exclusion, a contradiction, a near miss, or a credible foreseeable failure.

¶ Add before you remove. Prefer changes that add a usable option. Check later whether the option became compulsory through network effects, withdrawn services or cost.

¶ Test the successor first. Test it at a fitting scale, record what would count as its failure, and keep the old rule's protective functions. Stage changes; declare coupled changes as one revision.

¶ Extremis clause. Where shocks can arrive faster than a staged switch, the successor must be prepared and rehearsed before it is needed. That readiness has a standing cost, and the cost belongs in the decision. Emergency measures carry fixed expiry, independent renewal and review afterwards.

Reflexivity. A ranking, benchmark, forecast or diagnosis can change the behavior it measures. Tag each deployed measure R0 (negligible feedback), R1 (bounded or slow) or R2 (consequential), with reasons. For each, ask who uses it, what decisions follow, what behavior changes and what becomes invisible.

Ask also when it is taken. A measure on a known schedule invites preparation for the schedule rather than for the outcome (§4, D2).
