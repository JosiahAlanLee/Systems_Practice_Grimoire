§ 6

Irreversibility, habitats and artificial systems

Before irreversible destruction of something that sustains lives, compare feasible alternatives and the costs of inaction, and document the search. Three dials organize the burden:

¶ Irreplaceability: can the lost capacities be rebuilt in time?

¶ Imposition: who bears the loss, and can they contest it?

¶ Substitutivity: does an effective option disappear?

The dials share no unit and compute no verdict. Learning through trials also improves safety; precaution and experimentation both need their consequences assessed.

The strongest objection to this practice lives here. If one failure is unrecoverable, testing the successor first assumes a second attempt that may not come. The practice answers with the irreversibility burden rather than by dismissing the objection, so the disagreement moves to estimates of probability and irreversibility, where it can be examined.

Habitats. Protect a forest through the lives and capacities that depend on it, without declaring the forest a subject. If artificial systems come to bear interests, their compute, energy and cooling compete with biological niches, and equal consideration supplies no rule for allocating between them.

Artificial systems. A highly competent optimizer can be dangerous without revising its ends, and the practice offers no solution to alignment. It adds institutional questions: where the bottlenecks are, whether capability gains are weakening correction (D4), and whether safety measures are themselves being gamed. Technical corrigibility and institutional corrigibility are assessed separately.
