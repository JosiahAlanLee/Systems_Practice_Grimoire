§ 10

Worked example C: an analogy taken to its limit

The practice was also run on an unusual chain of comparisons: a mathematics preprint, slime molds and social movements. The example shows how analogy is used here and where it stops.

The chain. A preprint on computation in forced fluids describes two clocks. One slows continuously but still reaches every step; the other freezes between brief bursts while old signals fade below detection.

Slime molds and microbes show both patterns: steady activity in buffered refuges such as snow-covered soil, and dormancy between brief favorable windows. Social movements show both too: small organizations that keep networks and skills alive between upsurges, and shared rhythms that let dormant members resynchronize without sending messages.

What the practice did with it.

¶ Decided what it could audit. The fluid and the organisms have no owners, no standing and no measures that react to being measured, so only the human layer could be audited.

¶ Let the analogy prompt claims, not support them. The comparison suggested a two-layer design for coordination: public signals allowed to fade, durable capacity kept unmapped. Auditing that suggestion produced D1 (who controls the fading?) and D2 (who else reads the clock?).

¶ Accepted correction. A cross-review objected that the fluid "exactly" implements the two-layer split. The word overstated a partial parallel and was dropped.

What it cannot do. The biology validates no institutional claim. One pattern from the slime-mold literature is tentative: collective forms appear up to moderate extremes, while the harshest settings hold only solitary cells. If something similar held for people, legible coordination would depend on refuges where being visible is survivable. That is a question for evidence about people, not slime molds (§12).

The rule for sketches applies to analogies too: a sketch or an analogy prompts a claim and never replaces one (§13).
