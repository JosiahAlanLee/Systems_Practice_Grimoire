§ 9

Worked example B: a test that found nothing distinctive

In October 2026 the practice, in its Revision 3 form, was applied to six public postmortems: AWS S3 (2017), GitLab (2017), Cloudflare (2019 and 2023), GitHub (2018) and Facebook (2021). Each case got a competent site-reliability reading and a reading through the practice.

Result. The two readings found substantially the same mechanisms, and no finding unique to the practice emerged. On the variables that would distinguish it (retaliation against reporters, ownership of bottlenecks, incentives to game measures), all six reports were silent.

Why, by the practice's own audits.

¶ The corpus selects against the claims. A company's postmortem says what its publisher can afford to say. Governance causes appear where someone outside can compel them: regulator investigations, inquiries and litigation.

¶ The cases were famous. Both readings drew on lessons already absorbed from these outages, so they converged.

¶ One analyst wrote both readings with outcomes visible. That makes the run a feasibility check, not evidence.

What the practice concludes. The null result fits the declared scope (§1): a mature reliability review should leave little to add. It does not test the distinctive claims, which this corpus could not reach. The next run changes both the unit of analysis and the comparison:

¶ Study the dependents of single-owner failures, such as a widely deployed security update or a cloud region outage, to test the bypass audit and D1.

¶ Use regulator investigations of outages, which have an accountable recipient and named corrective orders.

¶ Use cases from after the reviewing model's training, or from organizations nobody writes about.

¶ Compare three arms: a competent review, the review plus the practice, and the review plus a generic checklist of equal length. Without the third arm, any gain may only come from asking more questions.
